Case study · Systems thinking

I planned a city five times before I understood grouping

A brief I set myself: 180,000 people, 40 km², a satellite city 45 minutes by rail from the nearest metro. Where does the factory go, and the school, and the courts? I kept redrawing until the reasoning stopped falling apart — and found I'd been arguing about interfaces the whole time.

01 · The brief

You can't call a layout wrong without saying what it was for

So I fixed the numbers first. Every draft below was rejected against these, not against taste.

180,000people at full build-out≈51,000 households. Big enough to need a plan, small enough to hold in your head.
40 km²developable landRoughly 8 × 5 km. Flat and greenfield — no terrain to blame.
4,500/km²average densityMid-rise, not towers. Between Portland and Amsterdam.
62,000local jobs, 90,000 workersThe other 28,000 commute out — which makes the rail link load-bearing, not a nicety.

Two constraints have a direction, and they decide almost everything. The prevailing wind runs west to east, so industry belongs on the east edge — anything upwind of housing means the whole city breathes it. The river enters at the north-west, runs south down the western boundary, then turns east below the city.

That second one is the interesting constraint. The water intake has to sit upstream and the sewage works downstream — but the works also has to be downwind. Only the south-east corner satisfies both, and once you see that, it isn't a preference any more. It's the answer.

You cannot fix downstream what you got wrong upstream. That sentence is doing a lot of work later in this page.

400 mto green space, for every home

60%of trips on foot, bike or transit

These figures are assumptions, not research — picked to be roughly plausible and, more importantly, specific enough to be wrong. A brief you can't fail isn't a brief.

02 · The drafts

Five layouts, four of them wrong

Each one was defensible when I drew it. What killed them was the reasoning, written down beside the drawing where I couldn't dodge it.

01

Pure zoning — one use per quadrant

Rejected

Why I tried it: it's the tidiest thing you can draw. Every type has its own place and nothing conflicts with anything.

Why it fails: tidy on the plan, unlivable on the ground. Nobody can walk to anything, so every errand becomes a car trip and the whole city commutes in one direction at 9am and back at 6. Sorting by what a thing is ignored what people are trying to do.

02

One magnificent central park

Rejected

Why I tried it: a single green mass is the most striking thing on the page, and it gives the city an obvious heart.

Why it fails: the park is a wall. It severs the road grid, so trips that should be straight go around it, and only the people who can afford the ring around it are actually near green. Six small parks serve four times the households — and photograph worse.

03

Radial — everything reaches the centre

Rejected

Why I tried it: perfect access. From any point on the ring you're one spoke away from the middle.

Why it fails: a trip from one suburb to its neighbour also goes through the centre. The hub absorbs traffic it has no business carrying, and chokes. Optimising the shortest path to one place quietly made every other path worse.

04

Industry at the interchange

Rejected

Why I tried it: factories need freight, freight needs rail, and the rail is already there. One piece of infrastructure, two jobs.

Why it fails: that station is also how 30,000 people get to work, so freight and commuters fight over the same platforms. And the cheap land east of the works — directly downwind — is exactly where housing ends up. This is the draft where I noticed the cost always lands on whoever has the least choice.

05

Transit spine, walkable cells

Adopted

What changed: I stopped grouping by type and started grouping by trip. Each cell holds what one household needs in a normal week, within a ten-minute walk of its own station. Offices concentrate at the one interchange that can carry them. Industry sits downwind behind a planted belt, on its own freight spur.

Four of the five were defensible on paper. What separated this one is that its failure modes are ones I'd be willing to explain to the people who'd live with them.

04 · The programme

A city is about seventy things, not eight

Once colleges, courts, banks, supermarkets and stadiums go in, "where does it belong" stops being answerable one building at a time. What worked was sorting every use by how often a household goes against how much land it eats — because those two questions, not the category name, decide the answer.

The corners are poles, not bins — weekly things sit near the middle by design. Read the diagonal: the further top-left something sits, the more it has to borrow accessibility from the transit line rather than generating its own. A stadium and a pharmacy are opposite problems, and calling them both "amenities" hides that.

On a screen: undo, error recovery, support, "cancel this payment". Frequency is the wrong metric — these are sized for the bad day.

On a screen: the tax-season flow, year-end close, onboarding. Rare enough not to earn permanent prime space, urgent enough to reach in one move when it spikes.

Necessary, unloved

Downwind, downstream, buffered — and never forgotten

On a screen: settings, logs, billing, data export, account deletion. Out of the way — and the ones that must never break.

And the land you deliberately don't build on

Beyond the belt, and along the water
Farmland & market gardens · orchards · woodland · the green belt itself · the flood plain, which becomes the linear park and the cycle route · a solar array on ground that floods · the airfield's noise contour · the quarry's haulage corridor
On a screen: whitespace, rate limits, and the features you decided not to ship. Empty is a decision, and on a plan it's the decision that's hardest to defend in a meeting and cheapest to regret losing.
05 · The limit

Two things I found I couldn't place at all

You can site a school. You cannot site a good restaurant.

Restaurants, cafés, salons, tuition centres, gyms, repair shops — none of these can be zoned into existence. What a plan can do is create the conditions: small cheap ground-floor units, footfall passing the door, and permission to change use without a fight. Then they appear, or they don't.

On a screen: exactly the same split. You place navigation, settings and the primary flow. You cannot place community, habit or word of mouth — only make them likely, by lowering the cost of the first post and putting people past each other's work. Confusing the two is how you get a "Community" tab nobody opens.

Plan the container, not the contents

I sized a high street around bank branches. Bank branches are disappearing. A plan that specifies twelve banks ages into twelve empty units; a plan that specifies flexible ground floor, 40–90 m², own entrance, changeable use becomes banks, then cafés, then clinics, then whatever comes next.

On a screen: build the slot, not the widget. A card system, a settings section that accepts new rows, an empty state that adapts — these outlive every specific feature you put in them. The most durable decisions I made were the ones that named a shape instead of a use.
06 · The transfer

Then I opened Figma and made the same four mistakes

Every rule I ended up with was a rule I'd already been applying badly to screens.

ON THE MAP
ON THE SCREEN

The school sits between the houses and the park

Not because schools and parks are the same kind of thing — because a parent's Tuesday afternoon runs through both. Adjacency is a claim about what happens next, and people read it as one whether you meant it or not.

⇄

"Export" belongs beside "Share", not beside "Delete"

Group by the errand, not the object type. And the thing nobody should hit by accident gets its own block, the way industry gets its own edge of town — that 10px divider is a green belt.

Height falls off as you leave the station

The most accessible land carries the most people. Density that ignores access just moves congestion somewhere it can't be absorbed.

⇄

One primary action owns the prime real estate

Weight decays outward from it. Four buttons of equal prominence is four towers and no station.

Don't route every trip through the centre

Radial layouts look like great access until a trip between neighbours crosses the whole city. The hub carries traffic it was never meant to hold.

⇄

Sibling screens need a door between them

If the only way from one task to a related one is back out to home, the home screen becomes a junction instead of a place — and every flow inherits two wasted steps.

Somebody lives downwind

Every layout has a worst position. Deciding where the factory goes is deciding who breathes it — and the honest question isn't whether there's a cost, it's whether it lands on whoever had the least choice.

⇄

Every layout has an edge case it punishes

The grouping that reads cleanest for the median user is usually the one that strands somebody — the person with sixteen cards, the one on a small screen, the one who only came for the thing I demoted to the third tab. Naming who pays is the part of the work that doesn't render.

"Where does the factory go" is the same question as "which user do I make click twice." I just answered it more carefully when it was a city.

The working wall

Distilled from the original board — what I started from, what kept breaking, and where it converged.

Started from

Eight zone types on a blank grid: homes, admin, schools, parks, factories, offices, stations, airport
Perry's 1920s "neighbourhood unit" — size a district by how far a child walks to school. I swapped the school for the station.
Rule I set early: write the reason beside every block, or it doesn't count as a decision

What kept breaking

Tidy plans were the worst plans. Every time it looked clean, someone couldn't walk anywhere.
I drew the wind backwards and had the whole city breathing the factory. Caught it four drafts in.
Kept planning for uses, not shapes — sized a high street around bank branches that are disappearing

Where it converged

Group by trip, not by type. That one swap fixed four separate problems.
Two directional constraints — wind and water — intersect at exactly one corner. That's not a preference, it's an answer.
Some things you place. Some things you can only make likely. Knowing which is which is the whole job.
