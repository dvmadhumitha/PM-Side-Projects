A design-thinking exercise

Our mentor gave us a blank site and asked us to plan a city

An exercise, nothing more — no product, no users, no brief. It turned out to be far less about buildings than about how you group things, so I finished it properly at home.

The exercise — plan a city for 10 lakh people, and decide where every one of its parts goes.

The first attempt: everything important in the middle, homes around it. No density, no distances, no groupings — those had to be earned.

The brief

One number chosen, everything else derived from it

I picked the population. Everything else follows from it, and every step is checked against a real city.

How the brief was derivedSix steps

Change the first box and every box below it moves. That is the only reason to write it this way.

So: where does a city put its hospital?

India's public health standards size facilities by population, which turns the argument into arithmetic: a sub-centre per ten thousand, a primary health centre per fifty thousand, a community health centre per two lakh, a sub-divisional hospital per five to six lakh. A district hospital does not appear until ten lakh.

At 10 lakh

One district hospital, two sub-divisional hospitals, five community health centres, twenty urban primary health centres and about a hundred sub-centres. A whole hierarchy, not a single building.

Which means

The answer is a threshold before it is a place — and it is five different answers at five different tiers. Where each one goes comes much later, and turns out to be the easy part.

Working it out

Each drawing asked a question, and left me with more to place

Each failure forced me to name what was inside the thing that failed. Eight items became about seventy, and the groupings appeared along the way.

The method

Seventy things is not a list you can place

Six loose groupings that overlapped was not a plan. Sorting by behaviour rather than by category is what tidied them into eight.

Affinity clusteringTap a cluster

Tap a cluster to see what is in it. Sorted by how often people go against how much room it takes — not by what the thing is.

The two questions that did the sorting

Sorted by category, healthcare is one thing and gets one location. Sorted by how often people actually go, it is two: the hospital you visit twice in a decade, and the pharmacy you need on a Tuesday night.

How often

Every day, every week, a few times a year, or once in a lifetime and never by choice.

How much room

A shopfront, a block, or forty hectares with a buffer around it.

Result

Six loose groupings became eight clusters with one placement rule each. Eight rules is something you can draw.

The one I kept

Two spines, seven districts, fifty-five cells

Nothing placed by the drawing. Each cluster has one rule, and the rule survives the population changing.

Whole cityZoom in

Fifty-five cells in seven districts, on two lines. A repeated unit, not a centre with suburbs around it.

Where each cluster goes, and why

Inside one cell800 m

One cell at walking scale. Everything named here, the community hall included, is reachable without crossing an arterial road.

And the hospital, finally

On the most reachable stop, next to the college and the courts. Not because a hospital deserves the centre, but because whoever needs one has the least choice about going.

The cell

About eighteen thousand people — enough to keep a primary school, a GP and a few shops going without anyone driving.

Compared

Vällingby was built for 20–25,000 and Bhubaneswar's units for rather fewer. Mine sits between them, which is reassuring rather than original.

And its identity

A satellite where most of the peak leaves in the morning. So co-working in every cell, the stadium at the far end to counter-flow the commute, and hotels modest but sat on the station.

Precedent

Stockholm strung its towns along the metro and put work inside each one. Bhubaneswar proved the unit works, and that it has to be sized for the city you become.

Twenty years on

A plan has to survive the next hundred thousand people

Two per cent a year is deliberately conservative. Bhubaneswar was drawn for 40,000 and is past eight lakh, which is the more likely failure mode.

At 2% a yearProjection

Densifying the cells nearest the line absorbs the first decade. The second decade needs new cells, not a new plan.

Three moves, in this order

First

Densify the three districts nearest the interchange, by about a third — still less dense than Chandigarh. Land that already has water, drains and a line. Roughly 1.2 lakh people, no new ground.

Second

Extend both lines east, a district at a time, each hanging off stops the lines were always going to need. About 1.45 lakh each.

Third

Only past roughly 16 lakh, a third line north. Expensive, and worth naming now so nobody reaches for it in year six.

What gives way first is never the street layout — it is the things there is only one of. The district hospital has headroom to about twenty-five lakh, so it is not the constraint. The works corner and the peak headway on the lines are: both need land and capacity reserved on day one. Gurugram is what happens when they are not.

What it changes in practice

Navigation

Daily things earn a permanent home. Weekly things sit one level down, never two. Occasional things are reached by search — not a demotion, just where people look.

Flows

Error recovery is the emergency service of a product. Designed for the worst case, not the average.

The unloved

Billing, logs, data export, deletion. Placed early, or they end up wherever there was room left.

A bit about me

Senior Product Manager at a fintech startup, with corporate years before that — I've built for users in India and for global markets, and the job is the same everywhere: find the real problem before building for it.

Most things are the way they are for a reason, and I like finding out what the reason was and whether it still holds. This page is the after-hours version of that. Some of it is me solving something in my own life — Italian exams, finding a vegetarian meal in an unfamiliar city. The rest is curiosity about objects most of us stopped noticing. Redrawing one is the most honest way I know to find out what the original was solving for.
