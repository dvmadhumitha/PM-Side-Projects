Lesson 01 · Three kinds of euro

Who owes you the money?

The euro in your hand, the euro in your bank app and a digital euro are not the same thing, and the difference is not technology. A banknote is a promise from the central bank. The balance in your account is a promise from your bank, which is why the state has to insure it. A digital euro is the first promise, in software.

It is not a cryptocurrency, not a new currency, and not a bank account. It is cash, in the place cash has stopped reaching.

Check yourself

Your bank fails on a Friday night. Which of the three do you still have?

Cash and the digital euro. Both are a claim on the central bank, so there is nobody in between to fail. Your account balance is a claim on the bank: you get it back from the guarantee scheme, but you wait.

Lesson 02 · Fig 2

Why Europe wants one

The case is not that cards are expensive. It is that Europe does not own them. Thirteen of the twenty one euro area countries have no card scheme of their own at all: in those countries every card payment is routed, priced and ruled by a company headquartered somewhere else.

Check yourself

European law already caps card interchange. Why has that not solved the problem?

Interchange is only one of the three cuts, and it is the one that stays inside Europe. It goes to the issuing bank. The scheme fee is not capped, and routing is not regulated at all. Capping interchange made cards cheaper without making them European.

Lesson 03 · Fig 3

The dates, and the two fights

Two calendars are running at once. The law is in its final negotiation, with adoption targeted for the end of 2026. The Eurosystem is not waiting for it: it closed its preparation phase in October 2025 and is building now, on the assumption the law lands.

Two things are still unsettled, and both are about money, not technology
  1. How much you may hold

    Around €3,000 has been modelled and found safe, but no number is fixed. Parliament wants the Commission to set it; the Council wants to set it itself, by reinforced majority. The ECB says it will be decided closer to launch, to fit the conditions of the day.

    Why it is fought over: the ceiling is the maximum amount of deposits that can walk out of the banking system.
  2. Who pays the banks that distribute it

    The shape is agreed: issuing and acquiring banks pay no scheme fee and no processing fee, the merchant charge is capped, the fee between providers is capped, and for at least five years those caps sit near the average debit card fee. The level is not agreed. Italy proposed a €0.02 cap under €10 in September 2026.

    Why it is fought over: it decides whether distributing a digital euro is a business or a cost.

This is worth noticing as a pattern. The EU's open finance file, FIDA, is stuck on the same question, what data holders are owed by data users. In European payments regulation, the technology is rarely the hard part. Compensation is.

Check yourself

The regulation passes in December. Your CEO asks when you can launch.

You cannot. The ECB's Governing Council only decides on issuance after the law is adopted, the pilot runs from mid 2027, and the earliest issuance is 2029. Your 2027 roadmap is pilot integration. Anyone promising a launch date is reading a press release, not the calendar.

Lesson 04 · Figs 4 and 5

Who stops getting paid

Two design choices carry almost all of the economics. A ceiling on what a person may hold, so money cannot drain out of the banks. And a fee model that removes the scheme fee entirely, which is the card networks’ revenue on every payment they carry.

Check yourself

Why does a bank care so much about a number like €3,000?

Because deposits fund lending. The holding limit is the legal ceiling on how much of a bank's deposit base can move to the central bank, and a bank's balance sheet is built on the assumption that most of it stays. A low ceiling makes the digital euro a wallet. A high one makes it a competitor.

Check yourself

A shop says one payment was taken from it twice. Which two pieces do you look at first?

The ledger and the queue. Either the instruction arrived twice and the idempotency key did not catch it, or it arrived once and was processed twice downstream. Those are different bugs with different fixes, and the event log is what tells them apart.

Through a PM’s lens
“The regulation passes in December. What do you build first?”Drawn from the Lead Architect (Payments) brief, Fabrick, Oct 2026

Fabrick is the open finance company of Italy’s Sella banking group. It does not sell payments to shoppers. It sells banks the connection to things like this, which makes the integration itself the product. It is hiring an architect specifically to join banks to the digital euro.

This is the awkward shape of a regulated build: the deadline is real and the specification is not. The pilot has a date. The holding limit and the fee caps are still being voted on.

So the job is to find the parts of the system that cannot change, the message formats, the security model, the identity checks, and get them finished while the parts that can change are still arguments.

Three moves
  1. Build to the rulebook, not the headlinesSequence the work so nothing on the critical path depends on a number still in trilogue. Formats and cryptography are safe. Anything priced is not.
  2. Make the pilot the deadlineExpressions of interest close on 27 October 2026 and the pilot runs from mid 2027. Those are dates on a calendar. 2029 is a hope, and you cannot run a team on it.
  3. Sell the connection, not the transactionYou cannot quote a per payment price before the cap exists. Price the integration per bank, so your revenue line survives whichever way the vote goes.
If this goes wrong

You ship a product whose unit economics are set by a committee you are not on. Price it on the payment alone and one vote in Brussels deletes the line. The same way a single regulator deleted the merchant fee on India’s biggest rail.
