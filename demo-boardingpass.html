Rethinking the boarding pass

One document, several readers, and part of its information changes after it is printed. This is a teardown of what the pass carries, who reads it, and when.

Context and assumptions

Scope fixed before any design work.

The task: deconstruct the boarding pass from the information out — classify every field by whether it was known when the pass was issued, then map which reader uses which field, and at what point in the journey.

The constraint: the passenger carries one artifact from check-in to the exit. It has to work at every checkpoint along the way.

Assumptions
  • Domestic travel within India, single segment, no connection.
  • Example route: BLR → DEL, IndiGo 6E, 08:15 departure, 2h 45m.
  • Booking is confirmed and a seat is assigned; the pass is issued at check-in.
  • Before check-in the passenger holds ticket details (PNR) and photo ID — not a boarding pass.
  • One checked bag (15 kg) and one cabin bag (7 kg), per domestic allowance.
  • A printed pass, since the study is about one physical artifact.

Stakeholders, and why three

The journey map listed everyone who touches the trip. Three were taken forward.
PassengerSecurity officerGate agent Terminal entry staffCheck-in / bag drop staffBaggage handlers Airline opsCabin crewLounge / retailMarketing partners

Shortlisting rule: keep the readers who make a decision about the passenger using the pass itself, at a defined moment. The others were set aside — airline ops and baggage handlers work from systems and bag tags rather than the pass; terminal entry checks the ticket and ID, since the pass may not exist yet; cabin crew, lounge and retail read it but nothing depends on the reading.

Passenger

Use case: orientation and wayfinding — where to go, by when, and where to sit. Reads the pass repeatedly across the journey.

Security officer (pre-embarkation check)

Use case: eligibility to enter the sterile area — confirming this person, today, is travelling on a real flight. One quick read, in a moving queue.

Gate agent (pre-boarding)

Use case: boarding authorisation and sequencing — whether this person boards this aircraft now, and in what order. A scan plus a glance.

Bar-coded passes became mandatory in 2010 (IATA Resolution 792), and a gate scan now records boarding directly. The design point is what the tear did: the artifact changed at the moment the passenger's status changed, and what remained was a smaller field set for the phase after boarding. In India the same idea existed as a security stamp — two of the three readers used to mark the pass. Today the pass carries one fixed field set from issuance until it is discarded.

What the sort shows. The two fields the passenger reads most often — gate and boarding time — are also the two most likely to change after printing, and they carry the same visual weight as fields that never change. Meanwhile some fields that are fixed at issuance and used after landing, such as the baggage tag reference, are attached as a separate sticker rather than designed into the pass.

The working board

How the analysis progressed.

Reference points

Collected real passes — domestic, and connecting itineraries
Listed every stakeholder in the journey, then shortlisted the three who read the pass to make a decision

Observations

Static and changeable info printed at the same weight
PNR and gate set in the same size — no anchor for the eye
Each reader uses a different subset; only name and flight are shared

Converging

Sorted every field: known at issuance vs not
Type weight set by the next decision the reader makes
Changeable fields moved behind a scannable live layer

ProcessWhat I did

  1. Mapped one route end to end (BLR → DEL) and listed every point where the pass is read.
  2. Listed all stakeholders, then shortlisted three using a stated rule, and wrote the use case for each.
  3. Classified every field three ways — static, dynamic, and static-that-changes — against what was known at issuance.
  4. Iterated layouts, sizing each field by the decision the reader makes next, and moving changeable fields to a live layer.

TransferableIf one artifact serves several readers

  • List every reader, then write the next decision each one makes from it.
  • Separate what can change from what cannot, and give changeable fields a live channel.
  • Shortlist stakeholders with a stated rule, so the exclusions are defensible.
  • Ask what the artifact should say in the phase after the one it was designed for.
