01 · Where it breaks

The edge cases are the everyday cases

The same plate hangs in a high rise, a hospital and a mall. It asks one thing, which way you are going, and answers nothing: not whether the lift heard you, not where it is, not how long you will wait.

The rider means "come and get me" or "I want floor nine". The panel hears "up" or "down". The gap between the two is the whole problem. It is a mental model mismatch, not a user error, and reframing it that way changed what needed building: from "people press wrong" to "the interface asks the wrong question".

  • Both arrows pressed, to be safe. Two hall calls, one rider, one wasted stop.
  • Pressed again, to hurry it up. Nothing changes, and the rider cannot tell.
  • A lit button while the lift is parked, meaning nothing. Downtime is undesigned.
  • A bank of four lifts, and no way to know which one answered your call.
  • Silence reads as broken.
02 · The rebuilt panel

One call, and four things the panel must know

Before pixels, the prerequisites: the lift's position, whether it is static or moving, its direction, and a tentative wait. The panel shows those and takes one input, a call. Direction is the controller's job once it knows where you are.

Every press gets a state, and every state is visible: acknowledged, en route, arriving. A first in, first out queue sits behind it, because fairness a lobby full of people can see is fairness they trust. In a multilift bank, the display names the door.

03 · What it costs, what it fixes

A display on every landing, and one button to learn

It costs a display per landing, a controller that exposes its queue, and the habit of pressing arrows. In an existing building that is a retrofit, which is my inference rather than something the exercise priced. It removes the double press, the repress and the hover near the door.

Music, games and countdown chatter were parked for a second version. Priority tokens were kept for hospitals.

The same move works on any product people keep "using wrong". Write down what the interface asks and what users think it does. That gap is the real specification. List what the system knows that the user cannot see, then expose the one piece of state that kills the biggest doubt. Give every action an acknowledgement. Design the boring states first: downtime, queues, failure. Delight is a v2 problem.
