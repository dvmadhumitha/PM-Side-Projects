The exploration wall

Distilled from the working Miro board — reference points → what we observed → how we converged.

Reference points

The classic "elevator button conundrum" UX debate — direction buttons vs summon buttons
Three very different homes for the same panel: high-rise apartments, multi-specialty hospitals, malls & offices
Watched real behaviour at the lobby: press both arrows, press again "to hurry it up", hover near the door

What we observed

Mental-model mismatch: the button asks your direction, people think they're calling the lift
Zero feedback after the press — is it coming? where is it? how long?
Downtime is undesigned: what does a lit button even mean when the lift is parked?
Multi-lift banks make it worse — which one answered your call?

Converging

Prerequisites locked first: position + static/moving + direction + tentative wait
FIFO queue for the controller — fairness a lobby full of people can trust
Feedback at every state: acknowledged → en route → arriving
Parked for v2: music, games, countdown chatter; priority tokens kept for hospitals

What this demonstrates

Separate user intent from system control. Riders don't want to declare a direction — they want to know where the lift is, which way it's heading, and how long they'll wait. The rebuilt panel acknowledges every request (FIFO queue), shows live position, and estimates arrival — the three prerequisites identified in the full exploration. Edge cases handled there: multi-lift dispatch, hospital priority, downtime defaults.

Behind the scenesWhat I actually did

  1. Watched lobbies before touching a whiteboard — collected the repeat behaviours: double-arrow presses, re-presses, the hover-near-door shuffle.
  2. Reframed the problem from "people press wrong" to "the interface asks a question users aren't answering" — a mental-model gap, not a user error.
  3. Specced prerequisites before pixels: what must the panel know and show — position, static/moving, direction, tentative wait — for both single- and multi-lift buildings.
  4. Designed the states, then the algorithm: acknowledged → en route → arriving, backed by a FIFO controller; wrote downtime and hospital-priority edge cases into the spec, and parked the delighters for v2.

Take this with youIf your users "misuse" your product

  • Write down what the interface asks vs what users think it does. That gap is your real spec.
  • List what the system knows that the user can't see — then expose the one piece of state that kills the biggest doubt.
  • Give every action an acknowledgment. Silence reads as "broken".
  • Design the boring states first: downtime, queues, failure. Delight is a v2 problem.
