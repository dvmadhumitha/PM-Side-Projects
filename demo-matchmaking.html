What this demonstrates

Marketplace intuition, made visible. There is no setting where everyone wins — every lever reallocates pain between riders, drivers and the platform. The full case study (Zola Cabs) works through these matchmaking dynamics for Indian cab markets: supply incentives, demand shaping, and where surge actually belongs.

Behind the scenesWhat I actually did

  1. Framed the marketplace as levers, not features: supply on the road, demand rate, matching radius — and wrote down what each side actually optimises for.
  2. Worked the scenarios: peak vs off-peak, under- vs over-supply, tight vs wide radius — tracing who wins and who quietly pays in each.
  3. Located surge properly: as the pressure valve for under-supply, not a revenue dial — and noted where it just taxes riders without adding cabs.
  4. Packaged the reasoning twice: a structured case-study deck (Zola Cabs), and this simulator so the trade-offs are something you can feel, not just read.

Take this with youIf you run any matching system

  • Write the three-way trade-off explicitly — user wait vs supplier earnings vs platform efficiency. If you can't name all three, one is silently losing.
  • Decide which metric you'll consciously sacrifice at peak. Unconscious sacrifice is how supply churns.
  • Watch the users who never match — they don't appear in your averages, they just leave.
  • Simulate before you promise: even a toy model like this one exposes who pays for a policy change.
