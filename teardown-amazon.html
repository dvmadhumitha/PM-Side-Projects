How does Amazon know it can deliver tomorrow?

One order traced from the forecast to the doorstep, and the choice behind the date: one national stock, or copies in every region.

Best for
  1. Replacing what ran out, by tomorrowThe printer ink that ran out before a deadline. It already sits in your region, so the page can say tomorrow.
  2. Restocking the household every weekThe same few things, several times a week, with no trip to a shop. Ordinary items are what the forecast places nearby.
  3. Selling your goods on Amazon’s promiseA seller pays Amazon to store and ship, so the fast date sits beside the listing, which their own shop cannot promise.

Less suited toSomething needed within the hour, or that you want to try on first: a store counter, or someone else’s shelf today, wins there.

Who uses it
  • The Prime householdOrders several times a week. Wants tomorrow to mean tomorrow, and the box at the right door.
  • The last minute buyerThought of it today. Wants same day, and will buy elsewhere if the page says Thursday.
  • The rural customerUsed to wait longest. Wants the same date the city gets, and a driver who finds the house.
  • The marketplace sellerPays Amazon to store and ship. Wants the fast date beside the listing, and a fee that leaves a margin.

Part 1 · The promise

The promise: a date worked out before you buy.

How soon an item can arrive depends on where it already sits. In 2025, more than 13 billion items arrived the same or next day around the world.

Source · Amazon

PM angleThe date is the product. A slower promise kept beats a faster one broken.

Part 2 · The forecast

The forecast: stock placed before anyone orders.

AI models forecast where, when and which products will be wanted, and stock moves near those customers in advance. Amazon says its speed comes primarily from that placement.

Source · Amazon

PM angleSpeed is decided weeks before the click, by a forecast.

Part 3 · The regions

The regions: one country, split into eight.

In 2023 Amazon rebuilt its US network into eight connected regions, each with enough selection to work largely on its own, instead of shipping across the country when a warehouse ran out.

Source · Supply Chain Dive

PM angleShorter distances mean lower cost to serve, as Andy Jassy put it. Faster and cheaper together is rare.

Part 4 · The same day site

The same day site: closer than a warehouse.

Same Day Delivery reached more than 9,000 US cities and towns in 2025, and rural delivery stations became hybrid hubs on a $4 billion investment.

Source · Amazon

PM angleRural customers used to wait longest. Monthly same day customers in rural areas nearly doubled in 2025.

Part 5 · The last mile

The last mile: someone still rings the bell.

Independent delivery companies in Amazon’s partner programme do the final drive. Amazon says serious vehicle crashes fell by more than 23% in 2025, with AI flagging risky driving to their owners.

Source · TNW

PM angleThe easiest mile to plan is the hardest to run: weather, stairs and missing house numbers all land here.

  2. 2PromiseThe page shows a date from where stock sits.before you buy
    The date on the page is a contract

    The page could always show a faster date and win the click; keeping it is what the forecast pays for. Conversion on the promise and deliveries kept on it belong to the same owner, or one will be gamed against the other.

    conversion by promised day · deliveries kept on the promised day
  3. 3PickA building in your region picks and packs it.in region
    In region is the test of the forecast

    If the item is picked in your region, the forecast worked; if another region ships it, the promise survives at the cost Jassy named: distance. The share picked in region is the forecast’s report card, read daily.

    orders picked in region · cost to serve per parcel
  4. 4SortParcels are grouped by route at a delivery station.by route
    The station fixes tomorrow’s routes tonight

    Sorting commits each parcel to a route and a van before the driver arrives, so a parcel late here misses a whole day, not an hour. Cut off times at the station are where same day lives or dies.

    parcels missing the station cut off · routes built per station
  5. 5DriveA partner’s van runs the route.the last mile
    The hardest mile is run by someone else

    Amazon plans the stops, independent partners employ the drivers, and the customer blames Amazon. Safety and pay are the levers: $1.9 bn more into the programme, and AI that flags risky driving to the owners. Measure outcomes at the door, not stops per hour.

    safety events per 1,000 miles · stops kept in planned order
  6. 6DoorThe parcel arrives, or the promise breaks.the moment the customer sees
    Only this moment reaches the customer

    Thirteen billion arrivals in a year hide the ones that did not. A customer judges the whole network by one missed date, so the broken promise rate, split by region and weather, is the number that walks back up the chain.

    deliveries kept on the promised day · complaints per 1,000 stops

Step 1 decides most of the speed: an item already in your region can keep almost any promise.

Fig. 3 · The system behind a delivery promise
  1. The shop

    The page that shows a date before you buy.

    • Delivery promiseWorked out from where the item already sits and how far it has to travel.
    The page sells the date, not the item

    Shoppers choose the item for the date, so the promise is a conversion lever as much as an operations output. Its owner is tempted every quarter to show a faster date than the network can keep; the broken promise rate is the brake.

    conversion by promised day · broken promises as a share of orders
  2. The planners

    Software that guesses what people will buy, and where.

    • Demand forecastAmazon’s supply chain team, SCOT, forecasts demand for more than 400 million products every day.
    • PlacementDecides which products to stock, how many, and in which building, at every layer of the network.
    Four hundred million daily guesses need a budget

    The forecast runs for every product every day, so the question is not accuracy but what an error costs: a copy too many ties up cash, a copy too few breaks a promise. Placement is where the PM sets that asymmetry.

    forecast error on fast movers · stock turns by region
  3. The buildings

    Warehouses in eight regions, and stations near you.

    • Fulfilment centreOne of 750,000 mobile robots fetches the item so it can be picked and packed.
    • RegionsEight in the US, each stocked to serve its own customers.
    • Delivery stationParcels are sorted into routes for the last mile.
    Eight regions mean eight copies of risk

    Regionalisation trades one deep stock for eight shallower ones. A region that runs out ships across the map, which is the old network’s cost coming back. The PM for the buildings watches how often a region leans on its neighbours.

    orders fulfilled outside their region · stock cover per region
  4. The last mile

    A van, a route and a driver.

    • Route planningSoftware orders the stops. Drivers often change the order, and Amazon ran a research challenge in 2021 to learn from them.
    • Delivery partnersIndependent companies run the vans and employ the drivers.
    Drivers rewrite the route, so learn from them

    The software’s order is a draft; drivers change it, and Amazon ran a challenge to learn why. Treating deviations as data rather than disobedience is the stance to copy, and the one a routing PM has to defend inside the company.

    stops delivered in planned order · minutes per stop by area
In plain words

Like a bakery that bakes before the queue forms. If it knows what the street will want tomorrow, the bread is on the shelf when you walk in; if it guesses wrong, you wait for the next batch.

Design choices, and what they cost
  1. Forecast, then placeStock moves near likely buyers before anyone orders. The cost: copies of the same item, held in many buildings.
  2. Regions instead of one networkEight regions shorten the distance each parcel travels. The cost: each region needs enough stock of its own.

How it grew
The spike
2020: net sales rose 38%, from $281 bn to $386 bn, against 20% the year before. From 2023 growth settled at 11 to 12%, on a far bigger base.
What drove it
The pandemic. From March 2020 shops shut and the parcel became the shop. Amazon grew its fulfilment and delivery footprint by about half in one year to keep up.
How they iterated
2023: the US network split into eight regions so stock ships from nearer. 2025: over 13 billion items arrived the same or next day. September 2026: another $1.9 bn for delivery partners.

Points: Amazon Form 10K for 2020 and 2025.

The algorithm · Forecast
In plain words
A baker who bakes tonight what each street will buy tomorrow.
Each street needs its own forecast

The analogy puts the job at street level, not national: the same item sells differently by place. The promise is that tomorrow’s order is already nearby. A sudden local surge, a heatwave or a viral video, is what breaks it, so correction speed matters.

forecast error by region · days to react to a surge
It optimises
Keep the delivery promise while holding as little stock as possible.
The last points of reliability cost most

Keeping most promises is cheap; keeping nearly all of them means stocking slow sellers in every region just in case. A PM picks the kept rate the customer notices, then prices each extra point in stock, rather than chasing a perfect score.

cost of each extra point kept · stock of slow sellers by region
The dial
How sure to be before moving stock: too early ties up cash, too late breaks the promise.
Finance and retail pull this dial apart

Finance wants cash out of the buildings; the retail team wants every date kept. Neither can settle it alone, so the PM sets the line per category and tests it in paired regions, reading results across a full season, not one week.

promises kept by category · inventory value by region
Same idea
Its recommendations run the other way: item to item, since about 1997. Shoppers who bought this also bought that.
Suggestions create demand the forecast must meet

One system predicts what you will buy; the other nudges what you buy. A PM should join them: recommending an item that is not stocked nearby sells a slower date. The reusable pattern is to let the promise filter what gets suggested.

The PM lens

“How would you evaluate a delivery driver’s performance?”

Amazon PM interview · hiring manager phone screen, May 2024 · reported to PracHub, 25 August 2026
What the round tests

Whether you separate the driver from the route, the weather and the promise.

  1. Start from what the customer felt.On time, at the right door, undamaged, safe. Those are outcomes; stops per hour is only effort.
  2. Separate the driver from the route.Rain, stairs, rural distance and dense blocks change every number. The candidate who reported this question said weather turned out to matter a lot.
  3. Make safety the guardrail.A fast driver who brakes hard is not a good driver. Read the safety events before the stops per hour.
If this goes wrong

Rank drivers on raw speed and you reward rushing and missed doors, then pay for it in complaints, damage and crashes.

The decision inside

Put the item in your region before you buy it.

  1. StopArrives tomorrow, on something you only just thought of.Thirteen billion times in a year.
  2. NoticeIn one national network, a missing item crossed the country.Distance is time and cost at once.
  3. TradeoffKeep one stock for the whole country, or copies in every region.Less inventory, or more speed.
  4. DecideEight largely self sufficient US regions, with forecasts placing stock near likely buyers.What it cost: more stock, held closer, bought on a guess.
