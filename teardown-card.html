What happens in the moment you tap your card?

Five layers of one card, pulled apart, and the choice inside them: one number used everywhere, or a new code for every tap.

Best for
  1. Paying at a till in two secondsA supermarket queue where the tap must beep. Under €50 in Europe no PIN, and your bank carries any fraud.
  2. Paying abroad on the same cardA shop abroad, in a currency you do not hold. The same rails reach your bank at home, with no exchange desk.
  3. Paying from a phone with a tokenAdd the card to Apple Pay and it gets its own number. A lost phone means cancelling a token, not the card.

Less suited toA ₹20 chai at a stall, where the reader and the fee cost more than the sale, so cash or a QR code wins.

Who uses it
  • The tapperPays with a card or phone without thinking. Wants the beep in two seconds and never a decline in a queue.
  • The small shopTakes cards to keep the sale. Wants the money soon and the fee small enough that cash stops being tempting.
  • The travellerPays abroad on the same card. Wants it to work everywhere and a bill with no surprise on the exchange.
  • The issuing bankVisa’s real customer. Wants fraud caught without good customers turned away, and incentives for keeping its cards on Visa.

Part 1 · The face

The face: the number is the weak point.

The first digit names the network, 4 for Visa and 5 for Mastercard; the next ones name your bank; the last is a check digit.

Source · Banco de España

PM angleAnyone who copies the number can try to spend it online. Most of what is inside the card exists to make that number less useful.

Part 2 · The chip

The chip: a computer that signs each payment.

For each payment it produces a one time code, so data copied from one payment cannot be used again.

Source · Visa

PM angleThe chip does not hide your number. It makes a copy of one payment useless for the next.

Part 3 · The antenna

The antenna: power from the reader.

A coil inside the card picks up power from the reader’s field. It works within 2 to 5 centimetres of the symbol.

Source · Visa

PM angleIn Europe a tap needs no PIN up to €50, until €150 or five taps in a row. Convenience is a limit written into law.

Part 4 · The stripe

The stripe: the same data every time.

Swipe it and nothing changes between payments. Copy it once and you can copy the card, the problem the chip was built to solve.

Source · Visa

PM angleOld paths stay for the terminals that still need them, and fraud moves to whichever path is oldest.

Part 5 · The phone copy

The phone copy: a stand in number.

Add the card to a phone and it gets its own number, a token, kept in a separate secure chip. Apple never stores the real card number.

Source · Apple Support

PM angleVisa had issued more than 16 billion tokens by September 2025, over three for every Visa card. A lost phone means cancelling a token, not the card.

Fig. 2 · One tap, out and back
The request goes out
  1. 1YouThe card comes within 2 to 5 cm of the reader.the tap
    The tap hides every decision that follows

    Two seconds and a beep is the whole experience the customer judges. Everything downstream is invisible until it fails, so the budget here is time at the till, and the share of taps that get asked for a PIN.

    approval time at the till · taps that fall back to a PIN
  2. 2TerminalReads the chip’s one time code.the shop
    The reader sets the pace of change

    A new rule in the chip reaches customers only as fast as shops replace their readers. Whoever owns this step ships through someone else’s hardware, so every upgrade is a rollout plan with the acquirers, not a release.

    share of payments still on the stripe · readers that accept a tap
  3. 3AcquirerThe shop’s bank sends the request on.the shop’s bank
    The shop’s only phone number is here

    When a tap fails, the shop rings its bank, never the network. Merchant trust, fee disputes and payout questions all land with the acquirer, so a network PM learns what shops feel only through this partner’s data.

    declines seen by the shop · days from sale to payout
  4. 4NetworkVisa or Mastercard routes it to your bank.the rails
    The rails are judged on time and uptime

    Visa’s own product is this hop: carry about 8,900 payments a second and never be the reason a till waits. The network has no customer at the counter, so its PM measures the hop itself, and the fee charged for it.

    payments a second at peak · time through the network
  5. 5IssuerYour bank checks the code, the balance and the risk.yes or no
    The no is the costliest answer here

    Visa scores the risk in about a millisecond, but the bank sets the line. A wrongly declined customer pays with cash and remembers; fraud shows up weeks later. Both sides of that line belong on one dashboard, owned by one person.

    declines that were not fraud · fraud loss rate
The answer comes back
  1. 6IssuerThe answer starts back.approved
    An approval is a hold, not a payment

    The yes reserves money; it does not move it. Pending lines that linger, or holds that outlive a cancelled sale, are where cardholders complain, so the issuer PM owns how long a hold lasts and how it is shown.

    holds still pending after settlement · complaints about pending amounts
  2. 7NetworkRoutes the answer.
    A lost answer becomes a double hold

    If the yes stalls on the way back, the till times out and tries again, while the first approval still holds the money. Reversal handling is dull work that decides whether customers see one charge or two.

    timeouts on the return path · reversals as a share of approvals
  3. 8AcquirerPasses it to the shop.
    Here the day’s batch begins

    The acquirer logs the approval it will send in with the day’s payments. That batch sets when the shop is paid, so the cut off time and the fee it keeps are the acquirer PM’s two levers with merchants.

    days from sale to payout · fee kept per payment
  4. 9TerminalBeeps. You walk away.done
    The beep promises more than it delivers

    To the shop the beep means paid; to the system it means approved. The gap is days of float the merchant carries. Any product that touches payout timing should be designed from this moment back.

    days from sale to payout · payouts the shop queried

Only a yes or a no travels during the tap. The money itself moves afterwards, between the banks: clearing and settlement. Who keeps what along the way: A product manager’s map of payments →

Fig. 3 · The system behind one tap
  1. At the till

    Your card or phone talks to the shop’s reader.

    • Chip or phone walletMakes a one time code for each payment. A phone sends a stand in number, a token, instead of the card number.
    • TerminalThe shop’s reader, supplied by the shop’s bank.
    The newest path depends on the oldest partners

    A phone wallet is only as wide as the banks that have joined it, and the till accepts whatever is oldest. Owning this layer means persuading issuers and acquirers to move together, which is slower than shipping the wallet.

    share of payments by token · banks live on the wallet
  2. The shop’s bank

    Collects payments for the shop.

    • AcquirerThe bank or processor that gave the shop its terminal, and that will pay the shop.
    Merchant economics live in this layer

    The acquirer sets what the shop pays and when it is paid. Those two numbers decide whether a shop asks for cash, so acceptance, not approval, is the metric here, and the acquirer is the partner who moves it.

    shops accepting cards · days from sale to payout
  3. The card network

    Visa or Mastercard: the rails between banks.

    • RoutingPasses each request to the right bank and the answer back, in seconds.
    • ClearingLater, nets out what each bank owes the others, so fewer transfers happen. For this, the network takes a fee.
    Routing is the promise, clearing is the business

    Routing earns the right to clear, and clearing is where the fee sits: data processing is the largest line of Visa’s revenue. The debit case is the reminder that rails everyone must use get asked who set the price.

    processed payments · fee per payment
  4. Your bank

    Says yes or no, then pays later.

    • IssuerChecks the code, the balance and the risk, then holds the money: an approval, not yet a payment.
    • SettlementAfter clearing, sends the money to the shop’s bank, which pays the shop.
    The issuer owns the customer and the loss

    Your bank takes the fraud loss and the complaint, so it alone sets how strict to be. It is also the only party that knows you, which is why the threshold and the $18.3 bn Visa pays in incentives both point at the issuer.

    false declines · incentives as a share of revenue
In plain words

Like a cheque with a trusted middleman, at the speed of a tap. At the till only a promise travels: your bank says yes. The money follows later, when the shop sends in its day’s payments as one batch.

Design choices, and what they cost
  1. Approve now, settle laterThe till needs only a yes, so the slow part, moving money, waits for a daily batch. The cost: the shop is paid later, not at the beep.
  2. A network in the middleEach bank connects once, to the network, not to every other bank. The cost: fees, shared out between the banks and the network.
  3. Tokens instead of card numbersWith Apple Pay a device account number sits in a secure chip, and Apple stores no card numbers. The cost: it works only where the bank has joined.

How it grew
The spike
Fiscal 2017: processed payments rose 34%, from 83 bn to 111 bn, far above the roughly 10% of a normal year. Fiscal 2020 is the other outlier, up only 2%.
What drove it
A purchase, not a product. Visa bought Visa Europe in June 2016, so a full year of European payments joined the count. Underneath, growth stayed near 10% a year until the pandemic.
How they iterated
Tap to Phone, October 2020, made an Android phone a small seller’s terminal. Tokens, in wallets since Apple Pay in 2014, now outnumber cards. Visa Intelligent Commerce, April 2025, let AI agents pay.

Points: Visa Form 10K for fiscal 2018, 2020 and 2025, and operational performance data for fiscal 2026. Fiscal years end in September.

In plain words
A bouncer who runs down a long list in a blink, before the bank says yes.
A verdict nobody should ever notice

The bouncer works at the door, not after the party. That fixes the job: every verdict lands inside the tap, unseen by the buyer. A rule that needs a second look, or a call to the cardholder, breaks the promise.

decision time per payment · payments sent to manual review
It optimises
Stop fraud without turning good customers away.
The wrong decline leaves no trace

Fraud arrives as a chargeback with an amount on it. A wrongly declined buyer just pays another way, so that cost hides. A PM has to estimate the lost spend and weigh it in the same currency as fraud loss.

fraud caught per honest buyer declined · spend lost to false declines
The dial
The decline threshold each bank sets.
A new line runs in shadow first

The score is Visa’s; where to cut it is a choice about which customers to risk annoying. A bank PM can run a new line silently beside the live one, compare who it would have stopped, then move it by segment, never all at once.

declines by segment at each threshold · fraud rate in the test group
Same idea
Apple Watch alerts: score a signal, act above a line.
Every alert spends a false alarm budget

The watch faces the same trade with different stakes: too many alerts and people stop looking, too few and the one that matters is missed. For a PM the pattern travels: score, line, action, and a false alarm budget set before launch.

alerts acted on · false alarms per user
The edge
Every bank connects once; every tap carries a code used only once.
  • The chip signs each payment with a one time code, so a copy of one tap cannot buy anything again.
  • Each bank connects once, to the network; a phone gets a token instead of the number, so wallets ride the same rails.
  • Against Mastercard the edge is thin, the same model. Against UPI and cash it is reach: the same card works abroad.
AI inside

  • Apr 2025: Intelligent Commerce lets AI agents pay with tokens, inside limits the shopper sets.

Cardholders never see Visa’s fee

The cardholder pays nothing to Visa directly, so rewards funded by interchange decide which card comes out. That puts cardholders and merchants in direct tension: every point earned is paid by the shop. Issuers measure them on spend and on staying.

MerchantsEvery customer able to pay, at the lowest fee per sale.
  • 150 m+merchant locations

Acceptance is bought with the fee

Merchants judge the network on approval rate and cost per sale. They pay the interchange that funds cardholder rewards, which is why regulators cap it in Europe and why US retailers keep suing. A decline at checkout loses the sale.

  • ~14,500financial institutions on Visa, issuers and acquirers together
Banks can take their cards elsewhere

Issuers are measured on spend, credit losses and interest. They need fraud scores that stop theft without declining good customers. Their tension is with the network: a large bank can move its cards to Mastercard when the contract ends.

The network sells uptime above all

Acquirers sign up shops and route their payments; Visa connects them to every issuer. Both are measured on approval rate and time to answer. Their tension is with merchants, who push acquirers to route debit over cheaper rival networks where law allows.

What the round tests

Whether you can name every party, and keep the approval and the money apart.

  1. Name the five parties.You, the shop’s terminal, the shop’s bank (the acquirer), the network such as Visa or Mastercard, and your bank (the issuer).
  2. Separate approval from money.At the till only a yes or a no travels, in seconds, signed with the chip’s one time code. The money moves later, in clearing and settlement.
  3. Follow the fees back.The shop receives a little less than you paid; the gap is shared by the banks and the network. That gap is why some shops still prefer cash.
If this goes wrong

Mix the two journeys and you promise a shop its money the moment the till beeps, when the payout comes later. Payout timing is where merchant trust breaks.

The decision inside

Make every payment impossible to replay.

  1. StopTwo seconds at a till, and a card that never leaves your hand.The tap feels like nothing happened.
  2. NoticeA copied number spends like the real one, online at least.The stripe hands out the same data every time.
  3. TradeoffOne number everywhere, or a new code every time.Simple and copyable, or safe and full of machinery.
  4. DecideThe chip signs every payment with a one time code; phones get a stand in number, the token.What it cost: token vaults run by the networks, more than 16 billion Visa tokens for nearly 5 billion cards.
