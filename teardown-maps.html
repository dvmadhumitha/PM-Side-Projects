How does Google Maps know the traffic before you reach it?

One trip in five stops, from the route on screen to the forecast behind it, and why the time shown is a prediction.

Best for
  1. Arriving on time in unseen trafficAny drive long enough for traffic to change. Maps forecasts the road you will reach; a sat nav only knows the roads.
  2. Finding the turn in a strange cityTurn by turn on roads you have never seen. The route is ranked against gravel, closures and small roads, not just distance.
  3. Rerouting a day of deliveriesTrip after trip, where minutes are money. When trouble builds ahead a faster route is offered, and only when it saves enough.

Less suited toquiet country lanes at odd hours, where few phones report and the local who knows the road still wins.

Who uses it
  • The daily commuterDrives the same road every morning and opens the app only to see how bad it is today. Wants the arrival time and nothing else.
  • The stranger in townDoes not know the roads, so trusts the blue line completely. Wants the turn called early and the right lane named.
  • The professional driverDeliveries or rides, trip after trip. Wants the reroute to be right, because minutes are money.
  • The shop ownerAppears on the map and lives by the hours and reviews shown. Wants the pin in the right place and the fake reviews gone.

Part 2 · Your position

Your position: satellites outside, signals inside.

Outdoors the phone listens to GPS satellites. Indoors, where GPS fades, Google has used cell towers and public Wi Fi signals, as in its indoor maps of malls and airports from 2011.

Source · MPR News

PM angleWhere position is weak, everything built on it is weak. Indoor maps start as a positioning problem, not a drawing one.

Part 3 · The road graph

The road graph: every road cut into pieces.

Roads are split into segments joined at junctions. DeepMind grouped busy neighbouring segments into Supersegments and modelled them as graphs.

Source · Google DeepMind

PM angleThe model sees roads the way traffic flows, as a network, not as a list of streets.

Part 4 · Live speeds

Live speeds: phones on the road as sensors.

Aggregate, anonymous location data from people navigating shows how fast each road is moving right now. Google Maps passed 2 billion monthly users in 2024.

Sources · Google · 9to5Google

PM angleThe users are also the sensors. The product gets better because people use it.

Part 5 · The prediction

The prediction: the road you will reach, not the road now.

History shows how a road usually behaves at each hour, and live data how today differs. Together they estimate the road 10, 20 or 50 minutes into your trip. Graph models improved ETAs by up to 50% in cities such as Berlin, Tokyo and Sydney.

Sources · Google · Google DeepMind

PM anglePredictions lean on the past, so sudden change breaks them. In the 2020 lockdowns, Google reweighted its models toward the last two to four weeks.

Fig. 2 · From a search to an arrival time
  1. 1LocateThe phone fixes where you are.GPS, Wi Fi, towers
    Everything downstream inherits this fix

    Every later step trusts this one. A fix that drifts a street puts the speed sample on the wrong road and the blue dot on the wrong turn. Decide early what the app does when confidence is low: ask, or guess quietly.

    time to first fix indoors · wrong road reports
  2. 2CandidatesPossible routes are traced on the road graph.several routes
    How many routes is a product choice

    Tracing more candidates finds better roads and costs compute on every search. The PM sets how wide the net is. A missed candidate is invisible to the rider; a slow answer is not, so latency usually wins the argument.

    search latency · trips where a better route existed
  3. 3Speeds nowLive speeds come from phones on each road.aggregate, anonymous
    Coverage is uneven, and so is the truth

    Busy roads report many phones; a country lane may report none at that hour. Where samples are thin the colour on the map is a guess, and the PM should know which roads those are before promising a time.

    roads with too few samples an hour · location sharing opt outs
  4. 4Speeds laterHistory and live data forecast each segment ahead.10 to 50 min out
    The weight on the past is the dial

    Lean on history and the forecast is calm but slow to notice a new jam. Lean on today and it twitches. In 2020 Google moved that weight toward recent weeks, which shows it is a setting someone owns, and should date.

    eta error at 10 and 50 minutes out · error after a sudden change
  5. 5RankRoutes are compared on time and road type.gravel and closures count
    Fastest is not the same as best

    The ranking is where values enter the maths: how many minutes a paved road is worth, how much a reported closure counts. These weights decide what millions are told to do, so change them with evidence and a log.

    share who pick a route other than the first · arrivals within the promised time
  6. 6RerouteIf trouble builds ahead, a faster route is offered.during the drive
    A reroute must earn its interruption

    Each offer asks a driver to change plans at speed. Save little and most will ignore it; interrupt too often and they stop reading. Set a minimum saving, and watch how many offers are taken.

    reroutes accepted · minutes actually saved after a reroute

Step 4 is the trick: the time on screen is a forecast of roads you have not reached yet.

Fig. 3 · The system behind a route and an arrival time
  1. Your phone

    Shows the map and, while you navigate, reports how fast it moves.

    • Maps appDraws the map from small pictures and sends its position every few seconds.
    The phone pays for the sensing

    Sending a position every few seconds costs battery and data on the rider’s phone, not Google’s servers. Push the rate up and traffic gets sharper while navigation drains faster. A maps PM owns that rate as much as the map.

    battery per hour of navigation · data used per trip
  2. The map

    Pictures of the world, ready before you ask.

    • Tile serviceThe world is cut into square tiles for every zoom level, drawn in advance and served from copies kept near you, a content delivery network or CDN.
    Freshness is the price of speed

    Tiles drawn in advance load fast anywhere, and a new road waits until the next drawing. The PM decides how often tiles are redrawn and where, since a city that changes weekly needs it more than open country.

    days from a road opening to its tile · tile load time by region
  3. The route finder

    Finds a few good routes and times each one.

    • GeocodingTurns an address into a latitude and a longitude.
    • Route plannerFinds the few shortest paths, predicts each one’s time from live and past traffic, then ranks them with your filters, such as avoiding tolls.
    This layer makes the promise on screen

    Geocoding and ranking are where a typo becomes the wrong suburb and a filter becomes a longer drive. Own the failure cases: an address that resolves to the wrong place costs more trust than a slow forecast.

    searches ending at the right door · routes rejected by the rider
  4. The traffic memory

    What every road usually does, and what it is doing now.

    • Location serviceCollects position updates: the raw material for live traffic, new roads and closures.
    • Traffic predictionGraph neural networks forecast busy stretches of road, grouped into supersegments.
    The memory is the moat, and the liability

    Years of positions make the forecast hard to copy and make privacy the standing risk. Aggregation and anonymity are a product commitment, not a back office detail, so bring privacy in before a new signal is collected.

    forecast error by city · location sharing opt outs
In plain words

Like a taxi dispatcher with a wall map and a radio. The map is printed once and pinned up; the radio says where every car is and how fast it moves; the dispatcher picks a route by guessing how the roads will look when you get there.

Design choices, and what they cost
  1. Draw once, serve many timesMap tiles change slowly, so they are drawn in advance for every zoom level and copied close to users. The cost: storage, and a delay before a new road shows up.
  2. Users are the sensorsPhones on the road report their speed, so traffic needs no roadside equipment. The cost: privacy work; Google aggregates the data and keeps it anonymous.
  3. Predict, do not just reportEach path is timed for when you will reach each road. The cost: forecasts lean on the past and can break overnight, as in the 2020 lockdowns.

Where the users are

No published split. Google gives Maps users only as one world total, two billion a month in October 2024, never by country or region. The nearest proxy, Alphabet’s revenue by region for the third quarter of 2026, puts the United States at about 53%, Europe, the Middle East and Africa at 26%, Asia Pacific at 16% and the other Americas at 6%, which is where the ads are sold rather than where people navigate.

How it grew
The spike
2012. Seven years after launch, Google counted a billion people a month in June 2012. For the next eight years it said only more than a billion, until it announced two billion in October 2024.
What drove it
The phone. Maps shipped on the iPhone from 2007 and every Android handset from 2008, and free turn by turn navigation on Android from October 2009 made it the app you open in the car.
How they iterated
Its own iOS app, December 2012, after Apple dropped Google’s map: ten million downloads in two days. Waze bought June 2013 for live incident reports. Offline navigation, November 2015, for weak signal markets.

Points: launch February 2005; ABC News on Google’s June 2012 event; Google’s own blog, February 2020; Alphabet’s third quarter 2024 call as reported by 9to5Google.

In plain words
A taxi driver who knows every road and has had the traffic radio on all day.
Promise local knowledge in a strange city

The analogy promises someone who has seen today’s roads, not only the map. The job is a time you can plan around in a place you do not know. Sending one driver down a road that closed this morning breaks it.

It optimises
An arrival time that comes true.
A padded estimate comes true and loses

Pad every estimate and almost all of them come true, yet the route looks slower than a rival app and people leave. Track error in both directions: arriving late hurts trust, arriving early means the number was hedged.

The dial
Fastest or most reliable: a quicker road can carry more risk of a jam.
Risk appetite belongs to the trip

Someone driving to a flight will take a slower road that never jams; someone going home will gamble. Engineering can price the risk on each road. The PM decides the default, when to offer a choice, and tests both by destination type.

The edge
Traffic read from the most phones, and forecast for when you arrive.
  • Aggregate, anonymous positions from people navigating give a live speed for each road, with no roadside equipment and the widest sample.
  • Segments are grouped into supersegments and graph neural networks forecast each one 10 to 50 minutes ahead, from history and today.
  • Waze, also Google, hears of a fresh jam first from drivers; Apple, TomTom and HERE have fewer phones feeding their traffic.

The PM lens

“Google is weighing whether Maps should offer mapping inside buildings. Should it?”

Google PM interview · product design and strategy · reported to PracHub, 8 April 2025
What the round tests

Whether you find the hard constraint before you get excited about the feature.

  1. Find the constraint first.GPS fades indoors, so position has to come from Wi Fi and towers, and floor plans from the venue. Neither belongs to Google.
  2. Pick the places where minutes matter.Airports and big stations, where a wrong turn costs a flight. Google’s 2011 indoor maps began with malls, stores and 18 US airports.
  3. Make venues want to keep it fresh.A floor plan goes stale the day a shop moves. Give owners a reason to update it, or the map quietly lies.
If this goes wrong

A confident blue dot on the wrong floor sends people the wrong way, and they stop trusting the outdoor map too.

The decision inside

Predict the road you will reach, not the road now.

  1. StopAn arrival time that holds while traffic changes on the way.Two billion people a month lean on it.
  2. NoticeLive traffic only describes the road now. A drive lasts thirty minutes.By the time you get there, the jam has moved.
  3. TradeoffReport what is known now, or forecast what is likely.Certain and stale, or predicted and sometimes wrong.
  4. DecideCombine each road’s history with live speeds to forecast it, and reroute when trouble builds ahead.What it cost: forecasts break when the world changes overnight, as in the 2020 lockdowns.
