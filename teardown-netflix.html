How does Netflix start a film in seconds, and choose what you see?

The drawing follows one play, row to recommender. The decision turns on where the library sits: far away, or inside your internet provider.

Best for
  1. Finding tonight’s film within a minuteNobody has a title in mind. Rows built overnight for each profile offer a sure play; a search would want a name.
  2. Following a series on a weak connectionThe next episode starts before the credits, and a ladder cut per title steps the picture down before it freezes.
  3. Pressing play on a launch nightMillions press play at once. The title sits on a box inside the provider, filled off peak, so it starts in seconds.

Less suited tothe clip you already know by name: that is a search, and YouTube does it free.

Who uses it
  • The evening defaulterOpens Netflix because it is what the television opens to. Wants something good within a minute, not a search.
  • The series finisherFollows shows to the end. Wants the next episode to start before the credits do, and a word when a season returns.
  • The shared householdOne membership, several profiles, one bill. Wants rows that stay theirs and a plan cheap enough to keep.
  • The member on the plan with adsTook the cheaper plan to save money. Will sit through a few breaks for the price, as long as the film still starts.

Part 2 · The player

The player: it steps down before it freezes.

Every title is stored at several sizes. The player fetches a few seconds at a time, moves down that ladder before the picture freezes, and back up when the connection recovers.

Source · The Register

PM angleA slightly softer picture beats a spinning wheel. Stalls are the quality metric people actually feel.

Part 3 · The encodes

The encodes: each title gets its own ladder.

Since December 2015 Netflix cuts each title to suit its content. A cartoon needs few bits; for a fast action film, 720p can look better than 1080p at 4,300 kbps.

Source · The Register

PM angleFewer bits for the same picture means more people get good quality on a weak connection.

Part 4 · The box in your provider

The box in your provider: Open Connect.

Netflix built its own delivery network and gives its servers to internet providers free, filled off peak with likely titles. More than 18,000 run in 175 countries.

Sources · Netflix · Netflix, at HUNOG

PM angleShipping hardware sounds unlike software. It made streaming cheaper for providers and smoother for members at once.

Part 5 · The recommender

The recommender: most of what people watch, it suggested.

Recommendations influence about 80% of hours streamed. Members give up after 60 to 90 seconds of choosing, having looked at 10 to 20 titles.

    The page is finished before you arrive

    Rows are computed overnight and cached, so opening is instant but a day old. The PM decides which rows may be refreshed live and which can wait; each live row buys freshness with latency and compute.

    Ninety seconds is the whole page’s budget

    Every row competes for 10 to 20 glances. The PM decides what the first rows are for: a sure play, or something new. Tuned artwork lifts clicks, but a thumbnail that oversells is paid for in abandoned plays.

    sessions that end with nothing played · plays finished
  3. 3PlayThe app asks for the nearest copy.inside the provider
    The nearest copy is a routing decision

    Playback services check the account and licences, then pick the servers, before any video moves. The PM owns the wait from pressing play to the first frame, and what happens when the local box lacks the title.

    time to first frame · share of plays served from inside the provider
  4. 4ServeAn Open Connect box streams it.filled off peak
    Free hardware is a partnership to manage

    The box is a gift to the provider and a dependency for Netflix. A PM here is half an account manager: which providers get boxes, how many, and what to fill them with tonight. Guess wrong and the title streams from far away.

    share of hours served from the box · misses on launch nights
  5. 5AdaptQuality steps with the connection.a few seconds at a time
    Stalls are the metric, not pixels

    The ladder is tuned per title, so a step down costs less picture than it sounds. The PM sets how eagerly the player drops and how long it waits to climb back. Too timid stalls; too eager looks soft on a good line.

    stalls per hour · minutes at the top rung
  6. 6LearnWhat was finished feeds the next rows.the loop closes
    Decide what counts as a signal

    A click is cheap and a finished episode is not. The PM chooses which events train the rows, and the label chosen becomes the taste of the catalogue. Reward finishes and return visits, or the loop learns to make bait.

    plays finished · return within a week

Fig. 3 · The system behind pressing play
  1. Your screen

    Asks for a home page, then for a film.

    • Netflix appNative on phones, React on the web. It fetches video in short pieces and steps quality up or down.
    The app is the only part members see

    Every kind of screen runs this client, and each ages at its own pace. The PM owns the support list: which screens get the new player, and which keep the old one. A stall on an old device is still a Netflix stall.

    stalls by device type · share of plays on the latest player
  2. Netflix’s cloud

    Everything except the video itself, run on AWS.

    • API gatewayZuul: one front door that sends each request to the right service.
    • Home page cacheEach night a new home page is computed for every profile and kept in EVCache, a fast in memory store.
    • Playback servicesCheck the account and the licences, choose the files, and pick the best servers to send them.
    One front door is one place to fail

    Zuul sees every request, so a bad release here is an outage for everyone at once, before any video moves. The PM wants a release calendar for this layer and the gateway’s owner in the room on launch nights.

    failed play starts · gateway errors per million requests
  3. Inside your provider

    The film itself, a short hop away.

    • Open Connect serverNetflix’s own box inside your internet provider. Filled off peak, it serves the video and stores no member data.
    Nothing personal on the box is the deal

    A server inside someone else’s network holds video and nothing personal, which is what lets providers in 175 countries accept it. The PM protects that line: any feature that wants the box to know who is watching breaks the arrangement.

    providers with a box · share of traffic that leaves the provider
  4. Behind the scenes

    What was watched becomes what is suggested.

    • Event streamsKafka and Flink carry what members do.
    • StorageCassandra and other databases for member data; S3 for stored video.
    The event stream is where metrics are born

    Every number the PM will argue over, minutes to play, finishes, abandoned sessions, starts as an event here. If a client logs a finish differently, the recommender and the dashboard drift apart. Own the event definitions as carefully as the features.

    events lost or late · one definition of a finished play
In plain words

Like a restaurant chain with one head office and a kitchen in every neighbourhood. Menus, bookings and bills are handled at head office; the food is cooked around the corner, from ingredients delivered overnight.

Design choices, and what they cost
  1. Split the brain from the muscleControl runs on AWS; video runs on Netflix’s own servers. Each side does one job well. The cost: two very different systems to run.
  2. Compute the home page overnightRows for every profile are prepared in bulk and cached, so the page appears at once. The cost: a huge nightly job, and rows that move only as fast as it does.
  3. Fill the boxes while people sleepMost new video reaches the servers in off peak windows. The cost: guessing tomorrow’s favourites today.

How it grew
The spike
2020: 36.6 m memberships added in one year, 22%, the most Netflix had ever added, to 203.7 m at the end of December.
What drove it
The pandemic. From March 2020 homes under lockdown signed up in a rush, and most of the year’s additions came in the first half, before growth slowed again in the autumn.
How they iterated
Nov 2022: a cheaper plan with ads. May 2023: paid sharing, so a borrowed password became a bill; 2024 then added 41 m, more than 2020. From 2025: stopped reporting memberships.

Points: Netflix annual reports for 2018, 2020 and 2024, paid memberships at year end. 2025 is the more than 325 m Netflix gave in January 2026.

In plain words
A video shop clerk who rearranges the whole shop for each person who walks in.
The shop changes, the stock does not

The recommender cannot add a single title; it only chooses what you see first. So the promise is that something worth watching is always near the door. A front row that looks the same every visit breaks it, however large the library.

plays from the first row · titles shown again without a play
It optimises
The chance you press play and keep watching.
Tonight’s play against next month’s renewal

Pressing play shows up tonight; keeping watching shows up weeks later, when someone renews or leaves. Push the first and short, easy starts win. A PM reports both side by side and never ships a change that lifts the first while the second slips.

plays per homepage visit · members who renew
The dial
Relevance against variety: a row all of one genre bores people.
Variety loses every test run overnight

Measured on tonight’s plays, the model will always choose more of the same, because variety pays off later. So the PM owns the variety setting. Move it on held out groups and read the result over weeks, not on the first evening.

genres played per member each month
Same idea
YouTube: candidates first, then ranking, tuned on watch time since 2012.
What ranking rewards, makers learn to make

YouTube found that ranking on watch time rewarded long videos, whatever their worth, and it later added survey answers. The lesson carries: what the ranking rewards, makers learn to make. At Netflix the makers are its own commissioners, who read the same viewing data.

The PM lens

“You are a product manager at Netflix, asked how you would make its recommendations better. What would you do?”

Netflix PM interview · product design and strategy · reported to PracHub, 22 April 2025
What the round tests

Whether you define better for members before you touch the model.

  1. Define better as faster to a good choice.Members give up after 60 to 90 seconds. Measure minutes to play, and what share of plays comes from the first rows.
  2. Improve the picture, not only the pick.Artwork tuned to each member already lifts engagement. The same title can be offered to a romance fan and a comedy fan differently.
  3. Reward what was finished, not what was clicked.A tempting thumbnail abandoned after five minutes is a broken promise. Completion and return visits tell the truth.
If this goes wrong

Chase clicks and the rows fill with bait. Members click, quit, and learn to scroll past the recommendations.

The decision inside

Put the library inside the internet provider.

  1. StopA film starts in seconds, at full quality, on a busy evening.Millions press play at the same time.
  2. NoticeStreaming from far away is slow for members and costly for providers.The same popular titles cross the internet again and again.
  3. TradeoffRent delivery from others, or build and ship your own servers.Simple, or close to every member.
  4. DecideBuild Open Connect, give servers to providers free, and fill them off peak with what members will likely watch.What it cost: Netflix designs, ships and maintains hardware in 175 countries.
