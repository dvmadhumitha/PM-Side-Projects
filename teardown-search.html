How does Google pick ten links from the whole web in a fraction of a second?

The drawing follows one query, from pages fetched long before you type to the page of results. The decision turns on who gets to say a page is good.

Best for
  1. Checking a fact in secondsA train time, an Italian word, a shop’s hours. The answer often sits on the results page itself, before any click.
  2. Choosing what to buy or where to goReviews, maps and prices side by side. Ten results narrow a big choice to a short list of tabs.
  3. Getting to a site you already knowTyping a name instead of an address. The first result is the site, and the job is done in one tap.

Less suited toa question that needs one reasoned answer drawn from many sources: an assistant such as Gemini, or Google’s own AI Mode, is built for that.

Who uses it
  • The quick checkerTypes three words on a phone between meetings. Wants the answer on the page, not a site to read.
  • The researcherOpens five tabs from one query and compares them. Wants sources it can trust, not the loudest page.
  • The shopperSearches a product with “review” or “near me”. Wants prices, places and opening hours together.
  • The site ownerDoes not search so much as wait to be found. Wants to know why a page ranks where it does.
Fig. 1 · One search: played through, stage by stage
Part 1 · The crawl

The crawl: fetch the web, again and again.

Googlebot follows links from page to page and fetches what it finds. It cannot fetch everything every day, so it chooses what to revisit and how often.

Source · Google Search Central

PM angleWhat is never fetched can never be found. Freshness is a budget, spent page by page.

Part 2 · The index

The index: every word, pointed at its pages.

Fetched pages are parsed and filed by the words they hold. Google says its index covers hundreds of billions of pages and is well over 100,000,000 gigabytes.

Source · Google, How Search works

PM angleThe index is built before anyone asks. Work done ahead is what makes the answer fast.

Part 3 · The query

The query: what you typed, and what you meant.

Spelling is fixed, synonyms are added and the intent is guessed: a place, a fact, a product, a site. Since 2019 BERT reads the words of a query in context.

Source · Google, The Keyword

PM angleMost queries are short and vague. Guessing the intent well matters more than matching the words.

Part 4 · The ranking

The ranking: links first, then learned models.

PageRank counted a link as a vote, weighted by the rank of the page that cast it. Today it is one signal among many, and models such as RankBrain and BERT help judge relevance.

Source · Brin and Page, Stanford

PM angleWhatever is measured gets gamed. Ranking is a contest with the people being ranked.

Part 5 · The results

The results: ten links, and much more.

The page mixes links with ads, maps, images and, since 2024, AI Overviews. Ads are sold by a separate auction and marked as sponsored.

Source · Google, The Keyword

PM angleEvery box added above the links pushes the links down. The page is a budget of attention.

  1. 1CrawlGooglebot fetches pages by following links.links and sitemaps
    Crawl budget decides how stale a result can be

    A news page changes by the minute; a recipe barely changes in years. The crawler cannot revisit everything daily, so revisit rates are a product call. The PM owns the cost of a stale answer, and site owners feel it first.

    age of a page when shown · pages fetched but never shown
  2. 2IndexEach page is parsed and filed under its words.word to pages
    Deciding what not to keep

    Duplicates, spam and thin pages are dropped before anyone searches. Every page kept costs storage and slows retrieval; every page dropped can never be found. The PM sets the bar knowing site owners will notice before users do.

    share of fetched pages indexed · duplicate results per query
  3. 3UnderstandThe query is corrected, expanded and read for intent.spelling, synonyms, intent
    Intent picks the whole page

    A query read as local gets a map; one read as a fact gets an answer box. Getting intent wrong shows the wrong page, not just a worse order. The PM tracks queries that people rephrase at once, because each one is a missed intent.

    queries rephrased within a minute · share of traffic by intent
  4. 4RetrieveCandidate pages are pulled from the index.a short list, not the web
    Recall is set here, quality later

    Ranking can only reorder what retrieval found. If the right page is not among the candidates, no model downstream can save it. The PM watches recall on hard queries apart from the order on the page.

    good pages missing from candidates · candidates per query
  5. 5RankSignals and models score each candidate.links, words, freshness
    Each signal favours someone

    Links favour the established, freshness favours news, location favours the shop nearby. Weighing them picks winners and losers outside Google. Changes ship after rater reviews and live tests, and the PM signs off on the trade, not on the model.

    rater scores in side by side tests · clicks that come straight back
  6. 6ServeThe page is built with ads and features.under a second
    Speed is a feature people feel

    Every box above the links, an ad, an answer, an AI Overview, costs time to build and pushes the links down. The PM weighs each one against speed and against the clicks it takes from sites, which are why the web keeps feeding the index.

    time to results on slow phones · clicks out to sites per query

Step 5 is the trick: a page is judged by who links to it, not only by what it says about itself.

Fig. 3 · The system behind a search
  1. The open web

    Pages and links, put up by anyone.

    • CrawlerGooglebot fetches pages and follows their links, at a pace each site can bear.
    • Site rulesrobots.txt and sitemaps tell it where to go and where not to.
    The supply side is not paid

    Sites let Google fetch them because search sends visitors back. If answers on the page keep people from clicking out, that bargain weakens. The PM treats traffic sent to sites as a health number for the supply, not as a leak.

    clicks out to the web · sites blocking the crawler
  2. The index

    A map from every word to the pages that hold it.

    • Inverted indexFor each word, the list of pages that contain it, split across many machines and many data centres.
    Size against freshness

    A bigger index finds more of the long tail; a fresher one is right about today. Both cost machines. The PM decides where the next crawl goes: new pages, changed pages or deeper into known sites.

    results under a day old on news queries · long tail queries with no good result
  3. The ranking

    Many signals and learned models put the pages in order.

    • SignalsLinks, the words on the page, freshness, location and page experience, among many others.
    • ModelsRankBrain, neural matching and BERT help match meaning, not only words.
    Raters and tests are the eval set

    Google publishes guidelines for human raters who judge results, and tests changes live before launch. The raters do not rank pages; they say whether a change made results better. For a PM that is the lesson: define better before you ship.

    rater quality scores · wins and losses in live tests
  4. The results page

    Links, ads and answers, built for this query.

    • ResultsRanked links, then features: maps, images, an answer box, an AI Overview.
    • AdsA separate auction fills the sponsored slots, and they are labelled as such.
    The page that earns

    Search ads earn more than half of Alphabet’s revenue, and they sit on this page. The PM keeps a line between paid and ranked: ads are labelled and cannot buy a place in the links, or the trust that brings people back goes.

    ad load per query · ad clicks against result clicks
In plain words

Like a librarian who has read every book and filed every word on a card. When you ask, she pulls the cards for your words, then hands you first the books that other good books cite most.

Design choices, and what they cost
  1. Do the work before the questionCrawling and indexing happen ahead, so a query only reads a prepared index. The cost: keeping a fresh copy of much of the web.
  2. Links as votesRanking a page by the pages that point to it was hard to fake in 1998. The cost: a market in links grew up, and the fight against spam never ends.

Where the users are

No published split. Google gives no count of searchers by country. The nearest proxy, Alphabet’s revenue by region for 2024, puts the United States at about 49%, Europe, the Middle East and Africa at 29%, Asia Pacific at 16% and the other Americas at 6%, which is where the ads are sold rather than where people search.

How it grew
The spike
2016: two trillion searches a year, Google confirmed, after searches on phones passed those on computers in ten countries, the US and Japan among them, in 2015.
What drove it
Phones and defaults. Search came built into Android and Chrome, and by a paid deal into Safari on the iPhone, so a search box was always one tap away.
How they iterated
May 2012: the Knowledge Graph put facts beside links. 2015: RankBrain brought deep learning into ranking. Oct 2019: BERT read queries in context. May 2024: AI Overviews put a written answer above the links.

Points, Google’s own figures as reported: about 1.2 trillion for 2012, 2 trillion for 2016 and more than 5 trillion for 2025.

In plain words
A page is good if good pages point to it, like a paper cited by well cited papers.
Sell trust, not matching

The promise is that the top result is the one a person who knows would pick. A place in the links that money can buy, or a popular page that is wrong, breaks it at once, and people notice before any metric does.

It optimises
The most useful page first, for this query, now.
Two numbers that pull against each other

An answer on the page satisfies the searcher and cuts clicks to the sites that feed the index. Clicks out keep the web healthy, but can mean the page failed to answer. A PM sets a floor for each and reads them together.

The edge
An index of much of the web, ranked by links and learned models, served in under a second.
  • PageRank in 1998 judged a page by who linked to it, which the keyword engines of the day could not.
  • RankBrain from 2015, then neural matching and BERT, match the meaning of a query, not only its words.
  • Default placement in Chrome, Android and Safari brings the queries, and the queries teach the ranking; that loop is what rivals lack.

The PM lens

“How would you measure the success of Google Search?”

Google PM interview · metrics · a common practice question, not a dated report
What the round tests

Whether you pick a number that means the person was helped, not only that they clicked.

  1. Name the job first.People come to finish a task: a fact, a place, a purchase, a site. Success is the task done, not time spent on Google.
  2. Pair a usage number with a quality one.Searches per user can rise because results got worse and people tried again. Read them beside rephrased queries and quick returns from a clicked result.
  3. Guard the other side.Sites feed the index for the visits they get back. Track clicks out, so a gain on the page is not a loss for the web.
If this goes wrong

Optimise for answers on the page alone and sites stop publishing pages worth indexing. Results get worse a year later, where no dashboard is looking.

The decision inside

Rank pages by who links to them.

  1. StopSearch engines in the late 1990s ranked pages by their own words.So pages stuffed with keywords won.
  2. NoticeA link is a vote the author of a page cannot cast for itself.Far harder to fake than the words on your own page.
  3. TradeoffCrawl and store the whole link graph, or rank by text alone.A far bigger job up front, or results anyone can game.
  4. DecideWeight every link by the rank of the page that gives it, worked out over the whole web ahead of time.What it cost: a crawl of the web and its links before a single query (Brin and Page, 1998).
