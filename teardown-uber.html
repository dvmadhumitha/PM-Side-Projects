How does Uber set your fare and pick your driver in seconds?

Five stops from the fare on screen to the hexagon behind it, and why the price moves where cars are short.

Best for
  1. Getting a car after the last trainLate at night or in the rain, when the taxi rank empties. The price rises in your hexagon until drivers arrive.
  2. Catching a flight at a known fareLuggage and a fixed time. The fare is shown before you book, and the arrival time is corrected against real trips.
  3. Ordering dinner to the doorDinner at home, no car needed. The same hexagons and the same batch send a courier instead of a car.

Less suited tothe same trip every day at a steady price, where the bus is cheaper and never surges.

Who uses it
  • The late night riderOut after the trains stop. Wants a car to come at all, and will pay for it.
  • The airport travellerA fixed deadline and luggage. Wants the arrival time to hold more than the fare to be low.
  • The part time driverDrives when surge makes it worth leaving the house. Wants to see where the money is before switching on.
  • The eater at homeNever rides, only orders. The same matching serves them, for a courier instead of a car.

Part 1 · The app

The app: the price before the ride.

Since June 2016 riders see a fare, not a multiplier such as 2.1x. Surge has not gone away; it sits inside the price.

Source · TechCrunch

PM angleHiding the multiplier changed how surge feels, not what it is. A price people can judge beats one they have to multiply.

Part 2 · The hexagons

The hexagons: the city cut into cells.

Uber buckets requests and trips into hexagons, a grid it open sourced as H3 in 2018. Supply and demand are measured cell by cell; a hexagon is the same distance from every neighbour.

Source · Uber

PM angleThe grid decides what nearby means for prices and drivers. A choice about shapes became a choice about prices.

Part 3 · The batch

The batch: wait a moment, match better.

Uber waits a few seconds after a request so riders and drivers nearby can be paired together. The aim is the lowest wait for everyone, so the closest driver does not always get the trip.

Source · Uber

PM angleA few seconds of waiting buys shorter waits overall. Uber says matching saves ten years of people’s time every day.

Part 4 · The price

The price: higher where cars are short.

When requests outnumber drivers in a cell, the price rises, drawing drivers in and holding some riders back. Since July 2014 Uber has capped surge during emergencies across the US.

Sources · Uber · TechCrunch

PM angleThe same rise that clears a Friday rush reads as gouging in a storm. The emergency rule is part of the product.

Part 5 · The arrival time

The arrival time: a route, then a correction.

A routing engine adds up travel times along the best path; a model called DeepETA then corrects it from real outcomes, in a few milliseconds. ETAs feed fares, pickups and matching.

Source · Uber

PM angleThe ETA is not a display detail. It sets the fare and decides who is matched to whom.

  1. 1RequestYou set a pickup and see the fare.surge inside the price
    The fare screen is where riders decide

    Once the multiplier is hidden, the fare is the whole message. A rider who sees it and leaves is a lost trip the system never counts as demand. Measure the leavers, or surge will look like it always works.

    requests abandoned after the fare · fare shown against fare charged
  2. 2CellThe request lands in a hexagon.supply and demand
    Cell size is a pricing decision

    Too large and a quiet street pays for a busy stadium next door. Too small and a cell holds a handful of riders and no drivers, so the price jumps on noise. The resolution is the quietest lever in the stack.

    price swings between neighbouring cells · cells with too few requests
  3. 3BatchNearby requests and drivers gather.a few seconds
    Seconds of silence that have to be sold

    The rider stares at a spinner while the batch fills. The window buys better pairs, and every second of it must be explained on screen or it reads as a slow app. The PM owns both the window and the words.

    cancellations during the wait · seconds to a match
  4. 4MatchPairs are chosen for the lowest total wait.not always the nearest
    The city wins and someone waits longer

    Optimising the whole batch means some rider sees a nearer car drive past. That is a fairness story waiting to be told, and the PM needs an answer before it is. Watch the worst wait, not just the average.

    longest wait in the batch · driver cancellations after a match
  5. 5ETAThe route time is predicted and corrected.milliseconds
    One number, three products depend on it

    A wrong ETA misprices the fare, strands a rider at the kerb and spoils the match that trusted it. DeepETA earns its place as the busiest model because every error here is paid three times.

    eta error at pickup · pickups later than promised
  6. 6PickupThe driver arrives; the trip begins.the promise kept, or not
    The only step the rider remembers

    Everything before this was invisible maths. The rider judges the whole system on whether the car came when the screen said. A kept promise here forgives a high fare, and a broken one turns a fair fare into a complaint.

    arrivals within the promised minute · rider rating after a late pickup

Step 4 is the choice: the best match for the city is not always the nearest car for you.

Fig. 3 · The system behind a matched ride
  1. Two apps

    One for riders, one for drivers, always connected.

    • Rider and driver appsSend positions and requests; receive offers, matches and arrival times.
    • Push platformThe server sends updates the moment they exist, instead of apps asking every few seconds.
    Two apps means two customers to keep

    Every change to the rider screen has a mirror on the driver side, and drivers can leave for another app tonight. The push platform keeps both live, so an outage is felt in both markets at once.

    driver hours online · push delivery time
  2. The front door

    Every request comes in the same way.

    • API gatewayUber Gateway, built on NGINX: one entrance that routes each request to the right service.
    One door is one place to fail

    A single gateway gives the PM one place to add a check, a limit or a new market, and one place where a bad deploy stops every ride. Changes here are platform changes, and need every team in the room.

    requests failed at the gateway · minutes of outage a quarter
  3. The marketplace

    Prices, pairs and times.

    • Hexagon indexH3 cuts the city into hexagons; supply and demand are counted in each, and surge is set cell by cell.
    • MatchingWaits a few seconds to pair nearby riders and drivers for the lowest total wait.
    • Arrival timesA routing engine adds up the roads; a model called DeepETA corrects its guess in milliseconds.
    Three dials, one team, one outcome

    Cell size, batch window and ETA correction each move the others: a wider window changes which driver is nearest, and a corrected ETA changes the price. Tune them together, with marketplace science, or each team will undo the last.

    minutes to pickup · requests completed per cell
  4. The data

    Every trip and event, kept and streamed.

    • Event streamsKafka and Flink carry events as they happen.
    • Trip storeDocstore, built on MySQL, keeps trips so every service reads the same version.
    The record every dispute is settled against

    When a rider says the car never came, or a regulator asks what drivers earned, this layer answers. One version of each trip is what makes a fare refund or an employment ruling defensible.

    disputes resolved from the trip record · lag from stream to store
In plain words

Like a market square where prices are chalked up stall by stall. Each hexagon of the city is a stall; when buyers crowd one and sellers are few, the price there rises until more sellers walk over.

Design choices, and what they cost
  1. Push, do not pollApps used to ask every few seconds; at one point 80% of requests were those checks. Pushing saves batteries and servers. The cost: more than 1.5 million connections held open.
  2. Hexagons for a moving cityUber chose hexagons because people move, and hexagons keep the error small as they cross cell edges. The cost: every decision depends on picking the right cell size.
  3. Correct the map with learningThe routing engine knows the roads; DeepETA learns the gap between its guess and real trips. The cost: Uber’s busiest model, answering in milliseconds.

How it grew
The spike
2021. Monthly users fell 16% in 2020, the first drop Uber had reported, then rose 27% in a year to 118 m by the fourth quarter of 2021, the sharpest rise since 2018.
What drove it
Delivery. Eats kept the app open while the rides stopped, and when cities reopened in 2021 the same accounts rode again. Food brought the users in; Mobility brought the frequency back.
How they iterated
Uber One, November 2021, one membership across rides and food. Upfront fares for drivers, 2022, showing pay and destination before accepting. Uber Teens, May 2023, accounts for 13 to 17 year olds.

What sets it apart
The algorithm · Match
In plain words
A host who seats the whole room at once, not the first guest at the nearest table.
The guest can refuse the table

The job is a car for the whole room, not the first to ask. That works only if people sit where placed. Drivers can decline, and each decline sends a rider back to the next batch.

driver acceptance rate · riders rematched
It optimises
Short waits and completed trips across the whole batch.
Short waits need drivers sitting idle

The fastest pickups come from spare cars nearby, and spare cars are drivers earning nothing. Push wait times down and earnings per hour fall until drivers log off. Track both sides in the same review, or one market quietly pays for the other.

rider wait to pickup · driver earnings per online hour
The dial
The batch window: a few more seconds of waiting can make every pairing better.
A rider level test will lie

The right window differs by city and hour, so it is a market call. Riders share one pool of drivers, so testing by rider lets groups steal cars from each other. Switch the whole city between settings by time slot instead.

completed trips per hour · wait by time slot
Same idea
Any two sided market: riders and drivers, orders and couriers.
Batching pays only in a full room

With one rider and one car, seating the room at once changes nothing. The pattern carries to any matching market only where both sides are dense. Before copying it, count supply and demand per area per minute; in thin markets, nearest first is enough.

free drivers per open request · gain over nearest first
The edge
Prices by hexagon, matches by batch, arrival times corrected by learning.
  • H3 cuts the city into hexagons, the same distance to every neighbour, so supply, demand and surge are set cell by cell.
  • Matching waits a few seconds and pairs the whole batch for the lowest total wait; the nearest car does not always win.
  • Lyft, Bolt, DiDi and Grab match riders to drivers too; the edge is thin, mostly volume, since every trip teaches DeepETA.

The PM lens

“How would you measure success for surge pricing? What metrics matter?”

Uber PM interview guide · execution and metrics · CleverPrep, updated 12 August 2026; a prep site list, not a dated report
What the round tests

Whether you measure both sides of a marketplace, and the trust around it.

  1. Name the job of surge.Get a car to people who need one when demand spikes. Success is requests completed in surging cells, and minutes to pickup.
  2. Measure both sides.Did drivers move into the cells that surged? Did riders who saw the fare still book, or wait it out?
  3. Guard the trust.Complaints, cancellations after the fare is shown, and surge during emergencies, which Uber has capped since 2014.
If this goes wrong

Optimise for revenue alone and surge becomes a habit in the same places, until riders or regulators set the price for you.

The decision inside

Let the price move where the cars are short.

  1. StopA rainy Friday night, and a fare well above usual.Cars arrive anyway.
  2. NoticeWith a fixed price, a rush leaves most riders with no car at all.Demand jumps in minutes; drivers take longer to arrive.
  3. TradeoffA steady price with long waits, or a moving price with cars.Fairness as people feel it, or availability.
  4. DecidePrice each hexagon by its supply and demand, show the fare up front since 2016, and cap surge during emergencies since 2014.What it cost: the price feels unfair at exactly the moments people need a ride most.
