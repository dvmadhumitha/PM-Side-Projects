How does a message travel through a company that cannot read it?

One message, from the ticks to the server shelf. The decision turns on keeping the private keys on the phone.

Best for
  1. Sending a photo to the family groupThree generations in one chat, on any phone. The ticks say it arrived and who has read it; SMS cannot.
  2. Calling home from abroad for nothingOverseas on a weak connection. Calls and messages go over data for nothing, and a tick says whether it arrived.
  3. Answering a customer before they buyA small trader running a shop from a phone. Customers ask where they already are, and a quick reply keeps the sale.

Less suited tochat history that must follow you to a new device without a backup; Telegram keeps it in the cloud, which the lock rules out.

Who uses it
  • The family groupKeeps three generations in one chat. Wants photos to arrive, and the ticks to say so.
  • The small traderRuns a shop from a phone. Wants customers to reach them where they already are, and a reply before the sale goes elsewhere.
  • The one abroadMessages and calls home over data for nothing. Wants it to work on a weak connection, and to say when it has not.
  • The privacy mindedChose it for the lock, and reads the notice. Wants the promise kept when backups and a second device arrive.

Part 1 · The chat

The chat: three ticks, three moments.

One grey tick: sent. Two grey: delivered. Two blue: read. In a group, two grey ticks appear only when everyone has it.

Part 2 · Your keys

Your keys: only the public halves leave the phone.

At registration the phone uploads public keys: an identity key, a signed pre key and a batch of one time pre keys. The server never has the private keys.

Source · WhatsApp

PM anglePre keys let you start an encrypted chat with someone whose phone is switched off.

Part 3 · The message key

The message key: new for every message.

Every message is locked with a new key that ratchets forward, so earlier keys cannot be rebuilt from today’s.

Source · WhatsApp

PM angleSteal one key and you open one message, not a conversation.

Part 4 · The envelope

The envelope: what the server sees.

A locked box and where it is going. Since 5 April 2016 every message, call and attachment is encrypted end to end by default.

Source · Signal

PM angleThe content is private. Delivering it still means knowing where it goes.

Part 5 · The shelf

The shelf: delivered, then deleted.

Delivered messages are deleted from the servers. Undelivered ones wait, still encrypted, for up to 30 days.

Source · WhatsApp

    Who sent it, to whom and when is visible here, and that is what regulators and abuse teams ask for. How long that trail is kept is a product decision, taken with policy and security engineering in the room.

    Delivery is the promise, so time it

    The server’s copy disappears the moment this step succeeds, so a failed delivery cannot be inspected afterwards. A PM wants time to two grey ticks measured from the phone, in the field, not from a server log.

    delivered within a minute · stuck on one tick after an hour
  4. 4ThemThey open the chat.two blue ticks
    Blue ticks are social, not a log

    Two blue ticks tell the sender something about the reader, and the pressure to reply is the loop that keeps chats alive. A PM owns that default and its exceptions, and should expect some people to switch it off.

    share with read receipts off · replies within an hour of blue ticks
  5. 5OfflineIf their phone is off, it waits, still locked.up to 30 days
    Thirty days is a dial with two sides

    Shorten it and messages to someone who lost a phone vanish; lengthen it and the shelf fills with locked boxes that may never be collected. Worth checking how many deliveries happen after a week before touching it.

The server only ever holds a locked box. Each tick marks a delivery, not a reading by anyone in between.

Fig. 3 · The system behind one message
  1. Your phone

    Locks the message before it leaves.

    • Signal ProtocolEach message is encrypted with a fresh key on your phone; only the recipient’s phone can open it.
    A borrowed protocol, a promise you own

    The protocol is Signal’s; the promise is WhatsApp’s. Backups and a second device are the two places a key can leave the phone, so each is a product decision taken with security engineering, not a convenience feature.

    backups encrypted end to end · linked devices per account
  2. WhatsApp’s servers

    A sorting office that cannot open the post.

    • Chat serversKeep a live connection to each phone. In 2014 that meant about a million connections per server, on Erlang and FreeBSD.
    • Holding storeKeeps locked messages only while they are being delivered: up to 30 days if the phone stays off.
    • PushWakes a sleeping phone so it can collect what is waiting.
    The shelf is the only place to measure

    Content is dark, so the server sees arrivals, waits and departures and nothing else. That makes queue time and time on the shelf the operational metrics, and open connections the cost. A PM reads health from timing, never from what is said.

    time on the shelf · connections per server
  3. Their phone

    Unlocks it and reports back.

    • Decrypt and acknowledgeOpens the message and sends the ticks back: delivered, then read.
    The ticks come from someone else’s phone

    Acknowledgements travel back over the same locked channel, so a missing tick can mean a dead battery, a bad network or a bug, and the server cannot tell which. Telemetry from the app is the only way to separate them.

    acknowledgement lag · ticks never returned
In plain words

Like a post office that only handles locked boxes. It knows who sent each box and where it goes, keeps it until the recipient comes by, and throws away its copy once collected. It never holds the key.

Design choices, and what they cost
  1. Hold messages only in transitDelivered messages leave the server, so there is little to store or leak. The cost: history lives on phones and backups, not with WhatsApp.
  2. A connection for every phonePhones stay connected, so messages arrive at once without asking. The cost: millions of connections kept open at all times.
  3. Encrypt end to endThe server sees who talks to whom, not what is said. The cost: moderation has to work without reading content.

In plain words
A diary that changes its lock after every page.
An invisible promise has to be shown

Nobody can see a lock change, so the feature is felt only when it fails. The PM’s job is to make it visible: the notice in every chat, the code two people can compare. Any copy kept under one fixed key quietly breaks it.

security codes compared · key change notices shown
It optimises
Past messages stay sealed even if today’s key leaks: forward secrecy.
Count the exceptions, and the lost phones

Forward secrecy is not partly on, so a PM counts the exceptions: history sitting in copies outside the chain. Push that to zero and a lost phone takes every conversation with it. The two numbers move in opposite directions.

share of history in unencrypted backups · chats lost with a phone
The dial
Convenience against secrecy: backups and new devices are where keys can leak.
The default is where the dial sits

Most people never change a setting, so whether backups are locked by default decides secrecy for nearly everyone. Engineering can build both paths; choosing the default is the product call. Test the friction of the safer path, never a weaker lock.

The PM lens

“What do you think are the most important metrics for WhatsApp?”

Product sense and metrics case · reported to Interview Query, 6 June 2026
What the round tests

Whether your metrics follow the job people hire the app for.

  1. Start from the job.People use it to reach the people who matter, privately. So the north star is conversations, not minutes: people who message someone they know each day.
  2. Measure the ticks.Delivery rate and time to two grey ticks are the reliability metrics. A message stuck on one tick is a broken promise.
  3. Measure what encryption hides.Content is unreadable, so quality has to come from blocks, reports and people leaving groups.
If this goes wrong

Chase time spent and the app drifts from messages to feeds, the opposite of why three billion people opened it.

The decision inside

Deliver what you cannot read.

  1. StopTwo blue ticks on a message the company never saw.Three billion people a month.
  2. NoticeThe company that delivers the message cannot read it.Only public keys ever reach the server.
  3. TradeoffRead messages to catch abuse, or make reading them impossible.Oversight, or privacy by construction.
  4. DecideEnd to end encryption for every message by default since 5 April 2016; the server keeps only locked messages, until delivery or 30 days.What it cost: safety work has to happen without seeing the content.
