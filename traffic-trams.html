Why does a Milan tram wait at a red light?

At the 2024 morning peak, most of Milan’s trams ran at 9.5 to 11 km/h. Many European cities manage 13 to 15. Two things explain most of the gap: stops close together, and lights that rarely let a tram through.

01 · The rules

What does each lamp say?

Article 41 of Italy’s highway code sets three kinds of lamp at a junction like this one. Cars get the three colours everyone knows. Trams and buses get their own: a white bar on black, a shape no car lamp has. People on foot get a small figure that changes colour.

Amber has no legal minimum. A 2001 study for the ministry suggested 3 s at 50 km/h, or 4 s where long vehicles share the road, and the courts have upheld a 3 s amber.

02 · The wiring

Who decides when the light changes?

No one, second by second. A controller in a box at the corner runs a plan: how long each green lasts, and in what order. Sensors under the road count the cars. A radio on the tram asks for green as it comes. A control room picks the plan for each hour of the day and watches every junction wired to it.

The network was almost finished in 2011, and its priority for trams and buses was not switched on. “It needs a political decision,” ATM’s head of mobility systems told alVolante. In 2015 the city tried again on five of tram 9’s 25 junctions, aiming to cut its 7.6 km run from as long as 49 minutes to 40. Nine years on, the Politecnico’s transport lab found almost no priority beyond isolated trials.

03 · The wave

Why can’t every light turn green for the tram?

Lights along an avenue can turn green one after another, so traffic at the right speed never stops: a green wave. The delay between neighbours, the offset, is the distance divided by the speed. A wave fits one speed in one direction, and Italian lights, an urban planner told Linkiesta, have traditionally been set around cars. A tram is slower, and stops to let people on.

Five lights 300 m apart, the average spacing on tram 9’s route. Every light runs the same 90 s plan; only the offsets change. A car keeping to 50 km/h and a tram stopping to load cannot share one wave. Priority at each junction is what lets the tram keep its own time.

The PM lens

“How long does the average person wait at an intersection?”

What the round tests

Whether you decide exactly what you are estimating, build it from labelled assumptions, and then check it against the world.

  1. Pin down who, where and when.A driver, a tram rider and someone on foot wait different times at the same light, and 8:00 is not midnight. Pick one, say why, and say what you leave out.
  2. Model one light, then add the queue.Arrive at random and you meet red for the red’s share of the cycle, then wait half of it: red × red ÷ (2 × cycle). 45 s of red in a 90 s cycle is 11 s. Cars wait longer, because each also waits for the cars in front.
  3. Count people, not vehicles.A tram’s wait is every passenger’s wait. Counted that way, holding a green for a tram on a busy junction pays once about ten people are aboard: a seventh of a 35 m Sirio’s 71 seats.
If this goes wrong

Answer with one average and you hide the spread. Half the arrivals never stop and the rest wait up to the whole red, and the second half is what makes a tram line slow.

  1. StopA tram waits through a whole red while the side street empties.In the simulated hour, one tram waits 41 s at a single light. Holding the green 5 s longer would have saved all of it.
  2. NoticeThe wiring was built years ago, and has barely been used for trams.What was missing was never the technology. It was the decision.
  3. TradeoffEvery second the tram gains comes out of the side street’s green, and the walk across the main road.Holding a green costs the side street little. Cutting a red costs it about five times as much for each second the tram saves.
  4. DecideHold the green for every tram, up to 15 s. Cut the red only for late trams or a quiet side street, and never below 12 s for people on foot.What it cost: each car on a busy side street waits 1.0 s more when greens are held, and 8.5 s more when reds are cut as well. Someone has to defend that at the council.

The junction is a model, not a real one. Cars arrive at random: 600 an hour each way on the main road, and 200 or 450 on the side street. A tram comes every 6 minutes each way, and 150 people an hour cross the main road. A queue leaves one car every 2 s, about what traffic studies measure, and nobody turns. Each road’s green is in proportion to its traffic. The PM lens and the decision are practice answers written for this page.
